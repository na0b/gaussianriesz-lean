\begin{definition}[The local Hardy--Littlewood maximal function $M_2$]
Fix $n \in \mathbb{N}_0$ and let $\lambda$ denote Lebesgue measure on
$\mathbb{R}^n$.  For $w : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$
define
\[
  M_2 w(x)
    = \sup_{0 < \rho \leq 2}
        \frac{1}{\lambda\bigl(\overline B(x,\rho)\bigr)}
        \int_{\overline B(x,\rho)} |w(y)| \, dy
  \;\in\; [0,+\infty] ,
\]
the supremum being over real radii $\rho$ in the half-open interval $(0,2]$ and
$\overline B(x,\rho) = \{y : |y-x| \leq \rho\}$ the closed ball.  The integral
is the Lebesgue (lower) integral of the nonnegative function $|w|$ over
$\overline B(x,\rho)$ with respect to $\lambda$, taken in $[0,+\infty]$.

\emph{No Gaussian measure occurs here.}  Both the integral and the normalizing
volume are taken against Lebesgue measure, with no Gaussian weight in the
integrand and no Gaussian measure in the normalization, even though this object
is used inside the tablet's Gaussian development.  Its role there is to control
the \emph{local} Gaussian maximal operator on a fixed ball, where the Gaussian
density is bounded above and below by positive constants, so that no covering
theory for a non-doubling measure is needed.

\emph{The radius cap at $2$ is part of the definition, not a convenience.}  The
covering step used to prove the weak-type bound for $M_2$ extracts a disjoint
subfamily from a family of closed balls whose radii admit a common upper bound;
without a cap on $\rho$ no such bound exists.  Every downstream use is on balls
of radius at most $2$, so nothing is lost.

\emph{Values in $[0,+\infty]$, and no hypotheses on $w$.}  The supremum is taken
in the extended nonnegative reals, so $M_2w(x)$ is defined for every
$w$ and every $x$ with no measurability, local integrability or boundedness
hypothesis: an unbounded family of averages gives the value $+\infty$ rather
than an undefined or conventional value.  This is deliberate.  A real-valued
supremum would silently take a junk value where the averages are unbounded, and
a statement bounding such a junk value above would then be satisfiable without
any analytic content; the extended-real-valued definition makes every bound on
$M_2w$ an honest bound.  For the same reason the integrals are lower Lebesgue
integrals of a nonnegative function, which are defined for every $w$, rather
than Bochner integrals, which would be $0$ by convention on a non-integrable
integrand.

Finally, for every $x$ and every $\rho \in (0,2]$ the normalizing factor
$\lambda(\overline B(x,\rho))$ is a strictly positive real number: this is clear
for $n \geq 1$, and for $n = 0$ the space $\mathbb{R}^0$ is a single point,
every closed ball is the whole space, and $\lambda$ is the unit mass.  So the
quotient inside the supremum never involves division by $0$ or by $+\infty$.
\end{definition}
